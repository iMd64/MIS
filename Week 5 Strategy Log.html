Sears case & the two-way relationship

Why technology alone failed at Sears, the mediating box, and the two-way relationship between IT and organizations.

00:12:16to 00:16:28Slide 04–05Case • p. 110

Opening case: will Sears' technology strategy work this time?

Sears spent billions of dollars on cutting-edge information technology, customer databases, and automated logistics. Yet the company continued to lose market share to Walmart, Target, and Amazon, eventually plunging into bankruptcy.

Why did technology fail? The professor highlights that management defined a technology-first solution without reforming its store layout, customer service culture, employee incentives, or merchandising strategy. Technology is merely a tool; without organizational restructuring and customer alignment, massive IT spending produces zero competitive advantage.

00:16:28to 00:20:42Slide 06Two-Way • p. 111

The two-way relationship between organizations & IT

Information systems and business enterprises have a reciprocal, bidirectional relationship. While management decides what systems to purchase and build, the technology itself reshapes the enterprise once deployed, altering communication patterns, job roles, and operating costs.

Crucially, this relationship is not direct or automatic: it is mediated by a middle layer of organizational factors. Changing software code is easy; changing organizational culture, power distribution, and daily habits is far harder.

What is an organization: technical vs behavioral

Microeconomic production functions vs sociological balances of rights, privileges, and political friction.

00:26:06to 00:31:07Slide 08–09Figure 3.2 • p. 113

The technical microeconomic definition

Under the technical definition from microeconomics, an organization is a stable, formal social structure that draws resources (capital and labor) from the external environment and transforms them through a production process into finished goods and services, which are returned to the environment.

This model treats the organization like an input-output machine. Inputs are capital and labor; the production function combines them; and outputs are sold to replenish resources. This view is useful for analyzing capacity, scalability, and capital/labor substitution.

00:31:07to 00:35:12Slide 10–11Figure 3.3 • p. 114

The behavioral sociological definition

The professor turns to Figure 3.3: the behavioral definition views an organization as a collection of rights, privileges, obligations, and responsibilities delicately balanced over time through conflict and political negotiation.

Technical View (Figure 3.2)

  • Formal production function (inputs → outputs).
  • Focuses on capital, labor, efficiency, and scale.
  • Assumes change is easy: adjust capital or retrain labor.
  • Favored by computer scientists and software engineers.

Behavioral View (Figure 3.3)

  • Social structure of people, power, and norms.
  • Focuses on unwritten rules, job security, and status.
  • Shows change is difficult: altering systems threatens privileges.
  • Explains why technically sound software implementations fail.

Engineers often see organizations purely through the technical lens. But when software is deployed in a real company, the behavioral reality strikes: an ERP or dashboard that threatens middle-manager influence will be politically undermined, no matter how efficient it appears on paper.

Organizational features, politics, culture & structure

Routines, standard operating procedures, political friction, cultural fit, environmental threats, and Mintzberg's five structures.

00:35:12to 00:37:58Slide 12–13Figure 3.4 • p. 116

Routines & standard operating procedures (SOPs)

Routines are precise, standardized rules and practices developed over time to handle virtually all expected operational tasks. Related routines assemble into business processes, which in turn form the firm.

While SOPs create high efficiency and consistency, they create severe organizational inertia. When a new information system requires employees to change how work is done, workers resist because they must unlearn long-standing routines.

00:37:58to 00:39:34Slide 14Politics • p. 117

Organizational politics & resistance

Politics inside a company refers to the competition between coalitions over resources, rewards, and influence. Because information systems inevitably redistribute power, authority, and control over operational data, they trigger political resistance.

For example, if an executive dashboard provides senior executives with real-time operational figures, middle managers lose their status as information gatekeepers. Consequently, middle managers will resist, delay, or sabotage the system to protect their organizational influence.

00:39:34to 00:41:45Slide 15Culture • p. 118

Organizational culture: the invisible force

Organizational culture consists of fundamental, unwritten assumptions about what goals the firm should pursue, what goods it should produce, and how work should be accomplished. Culture creates common understanding and minimizes daily friction.

However, culture can strongly resist technologies that challenge accepted beliefs. A collaborative, open culture adopts digital workspaces (Teams, Slack, shared drives) with ease; a culture rooted in internal rivalry and departmental secrecy will treat shared digital platforms with suspicion and hostility.

00:41:45to 00:44:48Slide 16–17Figure 3.5 • p. 119

Environments & disruptive technologies

No organization exists in isolation. Figure 3.5 shows environmental forces constantly acting on the firm: customers, competitors, suppliers, government regulations, financial markets, and technology trends. Environments change much faster than internal routines.

This gap breeds disruptive technologies (Clayton Christensen): breakthrough innovations that ride a low-cost, convenient technical trajectory. Initially, they may underperform incumbent products, but they improve rapidly until they conquer the market. Fast-followers with strong execution often win over first-movers (e.g. Apple with the iPod and iPhone).

Economic & behavioral impacts: transaction costs & flattening

Coase's transaction cost theory, agency theory, organizational flattening, and Leavitt's diamond.

00:48:06to 00:51:20Slide 22Figure 3.4 • p. 123

Transaction cost theory: why IT shrinks the firm

According to Ronald Coase's Transaction Cost Theory, firms traditionally grew large because conducting business internally was cheaper than searching for suppliers, negotiating contracts, and insuring against default in open markets.

Information technology drastically lowers market transaction costs: searching online catalogs, obtaining digital bids, and tracking shipments via APIs is fast and cheap. Because transaction costs drop, firms can shrink their physical headcount, outsource non-core work to cloud partners, and remain highly profitable.

00:51:20to 00:54:30Slide 23Figure 3.5 • p. 124

Agency theory: reducing supervision costs

Under Jensen and Meckling's Agency Theory, the firm is viewed as a nexus of contracts among self-interested agents (employees) who require monitoring by principals (owners). As a company grows, the cost of managing and supervising staff escalates rapidly.

Information systems reduce agency costs. Telematics, automated audit trails, and executive dashboards allow a small group of senior executives to supervise thousands of employees directly, reducing the need for intermediate supervisory middle managers.

00:54:30to 00:58:57Slide 24–26Figure 3.6 • p. 125

Flattening organizations

The professor contrasts the traditional tall hierarchy with a modern flatter organization. In a tall hierarchy, numerous management layers exist merely to filter and pass reports upward.

Traditional Tall Hierarchy

  • Many intermediate management layers.
  • Frontline workers have narrow decision rights.
  • Reports travel slowly up; approvals travel slowly down.
  • High administrative and supervisory overhead.

Flatter Organization

  • Fewer management layers; broad span of control.
  • Operational data pushed directly to frontline workers.
  • Eliminates delays between information, decision, and action.
  • Requires capable employees and clear responsibilities.
00:58:57to 01:04:38Slide 28–29Figure 3.7 • p. 127

Leavitt's diamond & managing implementation resistance

Why do employees resist new information systems? Harold Leavitt's Diamond demonstrates that an organization consists of four interdependent components: Technology, Task, People, and Structure.

If you change the technology without simultaneously redesigning employee tasks, updating job incentives, and adjusting reporting structures, workers will resist to protect their status and power. Diagnosing the root cause of resistance allows managers to build a supportive change management plan rather than blaming stubborn workers.

01:11:58to 01:15:48Slide 42–43Table 3.3 • p. 135

The Internet's impact on competitive advantage

The Internet inflates customer bargaining power by providing instant price-comparison engines and transparent reviews, reducing search friction and driving switching costs toward zero. This intensifies industry rivalry as geographical barriers dissolve.

To defend margins, smart companies use information systems to create switching costs—embedding account data, customized preferences, and proprietary loyalty ecosystems that make abandoning the firm inconvenient and costly.

Value chain, value web & network economics

Primary vs support activities, synchronizing multi-firm value webs, synergies, and platform ecosystems.

01:15:48to 01:19:39Slide 44–45Figure 3.9 • p. 138

The business value chain model

Michael Porter's value chain model categorizes firm activities into primary and support activities to pinpoint where information systems can add margin:

  • Primary Activities (5): Inbound Logistics, Operations, Outbound Logistics, Marketing & Sales, and Service. Directly related to creating and delivering the product.
  • Support Activities (4): Administration & Management, Human Resources, Technology Development, and Procurement. Infrastructure that enables the primary activities.

Delays in one activity cascade into others. Linking these activities via shared databases eliminates friction and accelerates order-to-cash cycles.

01:19:39to 01:23:24Slide 46–47Figure 3.10 • p. 140

From the linear value chain to the networked value web

The traditional value chain is strictly linear and focused on a single firm. In modern digital business, enterprises coordinate via a Value Web: a synchronized, customer-driven network of independent suppliers, distributors, carriers, and retailers communicating in real time.

Rather than pushing products down a rigid sequential pipeline, the Value Web responds dynamically to live customer orders, rerouting shipments and updating inventory across independent business partners.

01:23:24to 01:26:32Slide 48–51Network Econ • p. 142

Synergies & network economics

When merged companies link separate IT systems into a single shared database, they achieve synergies by slashing redundant corporate overhead.

In physical manufacturing, adding more labor leads to diminishing marginal returns. In digital platforms, network economics produces increasing returns to scale: the marginal cost of adding another user is near zero, while the value of the platform grows exponentially with each participant (e.g. Uber, Airbnb, eBay).

01:26:32to 01:29:16Slide 53–54Figure 3.11 • p. 144

Business ecosystems: keystone platforms vs. niche firms

Competition increasingly occurs between entire business ecosystems rather than standalone companies. Figure 3.11 highlights:

  • Keystone Firms: Dominant platform providers that set the core operating standards, app stores, and APIs (e.g. Apple iOS, Google Android, Microsoft Azure).
  • Niche Firms: Specialized app developers and accessory makers that build products inside the keystone platform.

Sustaining advantage, labs & quiz

Advantage erosion, strategic transitions, practical labs, and midterm exam preparation.

01:29:16to 01:31:50Slide 55–58Transitions • p. 146

Sustaining competitive advantage & strategic transitions

Strategic information systems rarely guarantee permanent advantage because competitors inevitably copy software and adopt industry standards (e.g. airline web check-in or bank ATMs).

Moving an enterprise from one business model to another is a strategic transition requiring socio-technical change management across job roles, incentives, and culture.

Practical labs & common pitfalls to avoid

The professor points out the practical lab on Slide 60 (analyzing strategic information systems) and reviews four fatal mistakes: assuming technology by itself solves business problems; ignoring middle-management political resistance; attempting to change technology without adjusting Leavitt's other three components; and failing to build customer switching costs.
