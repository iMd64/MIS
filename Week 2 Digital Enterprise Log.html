Opening & the socio-technical foundation

Audio/video check, course kickoff, the four central business questions, and the core definition of an information system.

00:00:00to 00:03:30Slide 01Foundations

Welcome to Week 1: building the course foundation

Dr. Kenan conducts an online audio-visual check and welcomes students to the first official topical lecture: MIS and the Digital Enterprise. He reminds the class that while last week was an orientation, today establishes the analytical framework for the entire semester.

He introduces the foundational axiom of the discipline: an information system is not merely a computer program or hardware terminal; it is a working arrangement connecting people, process, data, and technology so that an organization can operate, make sound decisions, and continuously improve.

00:03:30to 00:07:30Slide 01Guide § 1

The four recurring questions every firm must answer

Rather than treating technology names as miracles or threats, Dr. Kenan advises students to view every enterprise through four persistent operational questions:

00:04:151. WorkWhat actual tasks and business workflows must be accomplished to serve customers?
00:04:452. InformationWhat timely, accurate information is required by workers and managers to execute that work?
00:05:153. AlignmentHow do people, business processes, and digital tools fit together to prevent bottlenecks?
00:05:504. FeedbackHow does management monitor performance, detect errors, and adapt before failure occurs?

Answering these questions prevents companies from adopting expensive software that fails to solve operational problems.

The coffee purchase: what hides behind a transaction

The barista screen, POS telemetry, real-time inventory deductions, payment gateways, and the operational chain reaction.

00:07:30to 00:11:35Slide 02Coffee Case

A simple purchase hides an entire information system

Dr. Kenan presents the lecture's anchor case: purchasing a cup of coffee before class. To the customer, the transaction appears trivial—tap a credit card, wait three minutes, and receive a cup of latte. But inside the business, that single transaction triggers a complex digital chain reaction:

1. POS TerminalData Capture
Records item SKU, price, timestamp, branch ID, employee ID.
2. Payment GatewayAuthorization
Encrypts credentials, verifies bank balance, logs interchange fee.
3. Barista DisplayWork Dispatch
Displays beverage customization queue in real-time order.
4. Inventory EngineStock Depletion
Deducts 18g coffee beans, 250ml oat milk, 1 paper cup and lid.
5. General LedgerFinancial Close
Aggregates revenue, tax liabilities, and hourly labor costs.
00:11:35to 00:16:15Slide 02–03Chains of Failure

The chain of failure: why isolated software efficiency is not enough

Dr. Kenan poses a critical diagnostic scenario: “What happens if the credit card payment succeeds, but the kitchen display system drops the barista queue?” The customer experiences total service failure even though the bank terminal functioned flawlessly.

Similarly, if a barista uses whole milk instead of almond milk without updating the POS button, tomorrow's automatic supplier reorder will be distorted. Data generated at the lowest operational level dictates managerial supply decisions weeks later. A business cannot fix customer experience by buying better cash registers; it must govern the entire cross-functional information flow.

Part 3 of 8 · 00:16:15 – 00:26:00 · 9 min 45 s

MIS vs the IT department & the value of data

Why MIS is not just IT, organizational adoption barriers, and data transformation into managerial insight.

00:16:15to 00:21:00Slide 04MIS vs IT

MIS is not another name for the IT department

Dr. Kenan confronts a persistent corporate fallacy: confusing Management Information Systems with the Information Technology department. While the IT department maintains networks, server patches, and firewall rules, MIS encompasses how the entire enterprise harnesses information to create business value.

IT Department Perspective

  • Focuses on server uptime, software builds, and network latency.
  • Considers a project successful if the software deploys on time.
  • Maintains technical infrastructure and endpoint security.

MIS Perspective

  • Focuses on employee adoption, workflow friction, and profit margin.
  • Diagnoses why employees abandon systems despite zero technical bugs.
  • Aligns organizational governance, user incentives, and strategic goals.

A multimillion-dollar CRM fails if sales reps record client notes on personal spreadsheets. The code did not fail; the socio-technical system failed.

00:21:00to 00:26:00Slide 05Data Value

Data creates value only when it reaches a decision or action

Dr. Kenan dissects the progression from raw data to business action. A timestamp or product barcode by itself has zero commercial value. Data only creates value when it informs a managerial intervention.

  • 1. Raw Events & DataIsolated transactional signals: “Order #4092, 08:14 AM, 1 latte, $4.50.”
  • 2. Structured InformationAggregated and contextualized facts: “Latte sales spike 300% between 08:00 AM and 09:00 AM at Campus Branch.”
  • 3. Managerial DecisionOperational policy adjustment: “Schedule two baristas and pre-stage milk steamers starting at 07:45 AM.”
  • 4. Business Value & OutcomeRealized financial result: “Queue wait times drop from 8 mins to 2 mins; morning customer retention rises 18%.”

Systems thinking & the four socio-technical dimensions

Hospital appointment bottlenecks, systemic sub-optimization, the input-process-output loop, and the 4 dimensions.

00:26:00to 00:31:30Slide 06–07Systems Thinking

Systems thinking: avoiding local optimization traps

Dr. Kenan examines how optimizing one department in isolation frequently ruins overall enterprise performance. He illustrates this with a hospital outpatient clinic: if the scheduling department optimizes booking speed by double-booking patients, their departmental efficiency metrics look stellar—while the waiting room overflows, doctors suffer burnout, and medical errors escalate.

Systems thinking requires analyzing the entire workflow from boundary to boundary. An elite MIS analyst optimizes the complete customer journey rather than celebrating local departmental milestones.

00:31:30to 00:37:00Slide 08Feedback Loop

The input-transformation-output system with closed feedback loops

Dr. Kenan reviews the classic systems diagram: an enterprise takes inputs (raw materials, capital, labor, data), executes a transformation process, and delivers outputs (finished products, fulfilled services, customer experiences).

InputsResources & Data
Customer demand, supplier items, employee time.
TransformationBusiness Processes
Assembly, validation, packaging, customer fulfillment.
OutputsGoods & Services
Delivered orders, satisfied customers, revenue.
Feedback LoopClosed Telemetry
Defect rates, customer satisfaction scores, audit exceptions.

Without continuous feedback loops, an organization operates blind: it continues producing defective outputs until external market forces enforce bankruptcy.

00:37:00to 00:42:30Slide 09–134 Dimensions

Deconstructing the four socio-technical dimensions

Dr. Kenan walks through the specific responsibilities of the four dimensions that dictate system performance:

00:38:10People DimensionWho decides, who operates, who is held accountable?Defines user role permissions, employee incentives, change resistance mitigation, and analytical training.
00:39:20Process DimensionHow does work reliably flow across departmental silos?Standard operating procedures (SOPs), cross-functional handoffs, segregation of duties, exception routing.
00:40:35Data DimensionIs data an enterprise asset or unmanaged exhaust?Data quality, unique primary keys, standardized product taxonomies, single source of truth across branches.
00:41:45Technology DimensionDoes architecture enable or constrain workflow?Point-of-sale terminals, relational databases, cloud hosting, REST APIs, latency, and disaster recovery redundancy.

Part 5 of 8 · 00:42:30 – 00:58:15 · 15 min 45 s

The four levels of systems: operational to strategic

Transaction Processing Systems, Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and Executive Support Systems.

00:42:30to 00:48:00Slide 14–15TPS & MIS

Operational systems (TPS) & managerial reporting (MIS)

Dr. Kenan contrasts operational-level systems with managerial-level reporting. At the operational base, Transaction Processing Systems (TPS) track the daily, high-volume heartbeat of the firm: sales scans, airline seat bookings, ATM withdrawals, and payroll clock-ins. TPS must be fast, error-tolerant, and perfectly consistent.

One tier above, Management Information Systems (MIS) summarize raw TPS transactions into structured, periodic performance reports for middle managers: weekly sales by region, variance against budget, and inventory stock-out exceptions. Middle managers do not read individual cash-register tapes; they need summary tables showing where targets are missed.

00:48:00to 00:53:30Slide 16–17DSS & ESS

Analytical decision support (DSS) & strategic vision (ESS)

For complex business decisions where answers are not routine, firms deploy higher-tier systems:

Decision Support Systems (DSS)

  • Supports semi-structured analytical decisions.
  • Utilizes mathematical models, sensitivity analysis, and what-if simulations.
  • Example: calculating dynamic shipping routes during canal closures or pricing flight seats.

Executive Support Systems (ESS)

  • Supports completely unstructured, long-term strategic choices.
  • Features high-level executive dashboards with real-time KPI dials.
  • Integrates internal financials with external macroeconomic and competitive intelligence.
00:53:30to 00:58:15Slide 18Level Matrix

Connecting the levels: four different questions, one shared truth

Dr. Kenan synthesizes how all four levels utilize connected enterprise data to answer four distinct questions:

00:54:10Operational“What happened today?”
Order #1042 processed at 10:14 AM for $85.
00:55:05Managerial“Are we on target?”
Store sales are 12% below budget this week; stock is low.
00:56:15Analytical“What if we change?”
Simulation shows 5% price cut will lift volume by 18%.
00:57:20Strategic“Where should we go?”
Board evaluates expanding retail into Scandinavian markets.

Part 6 of 8 · 00:58:15 – 01:12:30 · 14 min 15 s

Enterprise systems, speed & digital transformation stages

Cross-functional integration, sensing and responding at speed, and the three stages of digital maturity.

00:58:15to 01:05:15Slide 19–20Enterprise Integration

Enterprise systems: dissolving departmental silos

Older corporations suffered from “islands of automation”: sales used one software program, the warehouse ran a second, and accounting kept records on a third. When a customer inquired about order status, nobody had a single clear answer.

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems replace these fragmented silos with a single central database. When sales enters an order, the warehouse instantly sees shipping requirements, accounting updates receivable balances, and manufacturing schedules replacement parts without duplicate manual entry.

01:05:15to 01:12:30Slide 21–22Transformation

Digitization vs. digitalization vs. digital transformation

Dr. Kenan clarifies three terms that corporate managers frequently confuse:

  • 1. DigitizationConverting analog physical information into digital format (e.g. scanning a paper invoice into a PDF file). The underlying business process remains identical.
  • 2. DigitalizationUsing digital technology to modify an existing business workflow (e.g. introducing electronic invoice approval workflows that automatically route PDFs).
  • 3. Digital TransformationFundamentally reimagining the entire business model and customer value proposition through digital capabilities (e.g. Netflix shifting from DVD rentals to global streaming subscriptions).

Companies that only digitize paper forms without reforming workflows fail to achieve genuine competitive advantages.

The GreenBasket case & digital capability diagnostic

Scaling bottlenecks at a 6-store grocery chain, symptom vs root cause, and the 1-to-5 maturity framework.

01:12:30to 01:18:45Slide 23–25GreenBasket Case

Case analysis: GreenBasket grocery scaling crisis

Dr. Kenan presents the applied diagnostic case: GreenBasket, a rapidly expanding regional grocery chain with six retail stores, introduces an online pickup and delivery application. Within three months, online orders skyrocket—and operational chaos erupts.

Store pickers spend 20 minutes hunting for missing items; customers arrive at pickup lockers only to receive out-of-stock cancellation alerts; and warehouse inventory counts disagree with store shelves. Management's instinctive reaction is: “Let's buy an expensive AI replenishment algorithm.”

Superficial Symptoms

  • Customers receive “item unavailable” text messages.
  • Store associates spend excessive time searching shelves.
  • Online store displays outdated item availability.

Root System Causes

  • No unified master product database across stores.
  • In-store cash register sales do not update online stock.
  • Store staff receive zero training on inventory reconciliation.
01:18:45to 01:24:45Slide 26–28Diagnostic • 1–5 Scale

The 1-to-5 digital capability diagnostic

Dr. Kenan introduces the evidence-based capability maturity scale used to diagnose organizations before investing in software:

LevelMaturity StageOperational CharacteristicsGreenBasket Status
Level 1Ad hocProcesses are undocumented, chaotic, and dependent on heroic individual effort.Inventory reconciliation (Score: 1)
Level 2RepeatableBasic procedures exist within isolated stores, but vary across locations.Store order picking (Score: 2)
Level 3DefinedStandardized organization-wide procedures and central data definitions documented.Point-of-sale scanning (Score: 3)
Level 4ManagedWorkflows and data quality are quantitatively measured and controlled via telemetry.Target baseline for Year 1

Dr. Kenan's golden diagnostic rule: “Fix foundational data and process ownership (Levels 1–3) before purchasing Level 5 artificial intelligence.”

Semester project framing, tools & weekly synthesis

Forming balanced project teams, free diagramming and data tools, and the Week 1 core takeaway.

01:24:45to 01:28:15Slide 29Project Teams

Forming balanced project teams: avoiding homogenous groups

Dr. Kenan advises students on assembling teams for the semester capstone project. He strongly discourages teams made up exclusively of software programmers or exclusively of marketing students: the highest-performing teams combine diverse analytical perspectives.

A balanced 4-to-5 person team includes: a business process analyst (to map workflows), a data modeler (to design entity relationships), a system architect (to specify software and APIs), and a project/change manager (to evaluate governance, risks, and ROI). He confirms that all modeling tools used in the course are 100% free or web-based.

01:28:15to 01:31:22Slide 30Recap • Synthesis

Weekly synthesis: improve the system, not merely the software

Dr. Kenan concludes the lecture by revisiting the core lesson: “Never attempt to solve an organizational crisis merely by installing new software.” Software without trained people produces user confusion; software without streamlined processes produces automated waste; software without governed data produces fast bad decisions.

He announces that next Saturday's session will cover Chapter 1 from the textbook: Information Systems in Global Business Today, exploring the six strategic business objectives of IT and the Ponsse forestry telematics case study. He wishes the students an excellent week and signs off.
