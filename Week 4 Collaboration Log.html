The TELUS case: social learning & culture

Canadian telecom workforce retirement risk, formal classroom training failure, video wikis, and cultural incentives.

00:06:15to 00:10:40Slide 04–05TELUS Case • p. 71

Opening case: TELUS embraces social learning

Dr. Kenan reviews the Chapter 2 opening case: TELUS, a major Canadian telecommunications provider. TELUS faced an existential workforce threat: a huge proportion of its veteran engineers and customer-service technicians were approaching retirement age. Traditional corporate training relied on bringing workers into physical classroom seminars—an approach that was outrageously expensive, painfully slow, and failed to capture the deep, unwritten tacit knowledge of senior workers.

TELUS abandoned rigid classroom curricula and launched a social learning platform: internal video-sharing channels, collaborative wiki repositories, and peer-to-peer discussion boards where employees post 2-minute video demonstrations of how to solve complex network repairs.

00:10:40to 00:16:30Slide 05Culture & Tools

Why technology alone was not enough at TELUS

Dr. Kenan emphasizes the vital socio-technical lesson: “If TELUS had simply installed video-sharing software and wikis, the project would have failed completely.” Software cannot force employees to share proprietary trade secrets if the corporate culture rewards information hoarding.

TELUS succeeded because senior management paired the digital platform with cultural incentives and organizational redesign: employee performance reviews rewarded peer mentorship; managers allocated dedicated work hours for content creation; and leadership actively modeled social learning. Technology provided the pipe, but organizational culture provided the water.

Business processes & cross-functional order flow

Defining business processes, flows of material/info/knowledge, Figure 2.1 order fulfillment, and IT process improvements.

00:16:30to 00:23:00Slide 06–08Table 2.1 • p. 73

What is a business process? Material, information & knowledge flows

Dr. Kenan defines a business process as a coordinated collection of activities required to produce a product or deliver a service. Processes represent how work is organized, coordinated, and focused inside an enterprise.

Processes are supported by three interrelated flows: flows of material (physical parts moving across factory floors), flows of information (orders, invoices, shipping tracking codes), and flows of knowledge (expert diagnostic routines and engineering rules). While some processes are confined to a single department (e.g. Accounts Payable within Finance), the most critical business processes cross functional boundaries.

00:23:00to 00:29:45Slide 09–12Figure 2.1 • p. 74

Figure 2.1: the cross-functional order fulfillment process

Dr. Kenan analyzes Figure 2.1, showing that fulfilling a customer order is not a sales task—it is an end-to-end cross-functional journey involving three distinct departments:

SalesOrder Generation
Captures buyer order details, price agreed, and delivery address.
AccountingCredit Check
Verifies buyer credit balance, authorizes terms, generates invoice.
ManufacturingAssembly & Pull
Pulls items from inventory or schedules factory production run.
Shipping & LogisticsDispatch & Track
Generates bill of lading, hands order to carrier, sends tracking API.

The professor stresses: “If accounting takes three days to approve credit, the whole order is three days late.” Information systems eliminate manual handoff delays by sharing order data across all three departments simultaneously.

Operational & middle management: TPS & MIS

Transaction Processing Systems (Figure 2.2 payroll), Management Information Systems (Figure 2.3 & 2.4), and exception reporting.

00:29:45to 00:37:00Slide 13–16Figure 2.2 • p. 77

Transaction processing systems (TPS): the operational foundation

Dr. Kenan examines the lowest management tier: operational management. Frontline supervisors need detailed, current data regarding daily transactions to keep operations running. They rely on Transaction Processing Systems (TPS).

Figure 2.2 illustrates a typical Payroll TPS: employee badge taps and hourly timesheets enter the system; the payroll master file updates deductions and tax withholdings; and the system automatically issues bank salary deposits, government tax reports, and managerial labor-cost totals. TPS is characterized by high transaction volume, rigid standard operating procedures, and internal operational focus.

00:37:00to 00:43:45Slide 17–20Figure 2.3–2.4 • p. 80

Management information systems (MIS): control & exception reporting

Middle managers do not need to inspect every individual employee paystub or sales receipt. They need summary numbers and performance trends. Management Information Systems (MIS) extract transactional data from underlying TPS files, aggregate the records, and generate periodic performance reports.

Dr. Kenan walks through Figure 2.4 (Sample MIS Report): a consolidated sales report comparing actual regional sales against planned targets. Its greatest managerial power is exception reporting: instantly highlighting product lines or stores where performance deviates significantly from planned benchmarks, enabling middle managers to intervene before fiscal quarters end.

Analytical decisions: DSS & executive dashboards (ESS)

Decision Support Systems (Figure 2.5 voyage estimation), Executive Support Systems (Figure 2.6), and drill-down analytics.

00:43:45to 00:51:15Slide 21–23Figure 2.5 • p. 84

Decision support systems (DSS): what-if modeling & simulations

When decisions are semi-structured—meaning they cannot be resolved by a rigid mathematical formula—managers turn to Decision Support Systems (DSS). Unlike MIS, which looks backward at what occurred, DSS looks forward, helping managers explore “what-if” scenarios.

Dr. Kenan examines Figure 2.5 (Voyage-Estimating DSS for maritime shipping): chartering managers enter cargo weight, fuel price fluctuations, canal transit tolls, and vessel speeds into mathematical models to calculate optimal shipping routes, vessel charter rates, and expected profit margins under volatile weather conditions.

00:51:15to 00:58:30Slide 24–27Figure 2.6 • p. 87

Executive support systems (ESS): senior executive dashboards

At the apex of the pyramid sits senior management (the C-suite). Senior executives address completely unstructured strategic decisions: “Should we acquire a competitor in Latin America? How will new carbon tax regulations impact our supply chain?”

They utilize Executive Support Systems (ESS). Figure 2.6 shows an ESS portal: it aggregates internal financial KPIs with external market feeds (stock indices, competitor filings, regulatory news) into visual graphic dials. Crucially, ESS offers drill-down capability: an executive noticing an aggregate margin drop can click the red KPI to drill down from global division → country → retail branch → individual product line.

01:00:151. ERPEnterprise Resource Planning: central database unifying manufacturing, accounting, sales, and HR into one source of truth.

01:06:30to 01:14:30Slide 33–39Intranets • p. 97

Intranets, extranets & e-business terminology

Dr. Kenan clarifies essential business networking infrastructure: Intranets are internal corporate websites accessible strictly to authorized employees to consult company policies, benefit portals, and training docs. Extranets are secure portals that extend private corporate systems to verified external partners, suppliers, and distributors.

E-Commerce vs. E-Business

  • E-Commerce: The buying and selling of goods and services over digital networks.
  • E-Business: The total use of digital technology to execute all business processes inside the firm.

E-Government

  • Application of the Internet and IS to digitally deliver public services to citizens (G2C).
  • Enhances transparency, citizen voting, tax filings, and license renewals.

Collaboration, social business & space/time matrix

Table 2.4 deconstructed: synchronous vs asynchronous, remote vs co-located, and enterprise social tools.

01:14:30to 01:21:00Slide 40–45Social Business • p. 99

Why collaboration and social business have become essential

Dr. Kenan highlights that modern work has fundamentally shifted: jobs increasingly require teamwork, intellectual problem solving, and global coordination across time zones. Organizations cannot survive through top-down hierarchical commands; they depend on rapid peer-to-peer collaboration.

Firms invest heavily in Social Business—using social platforms (internal wikis, Microsoft Teams, Slack, enterprise social networks) to connect employees with shared interests, discover subject-matter experts, and accelerate innovation.

01:21:00to 01:26:45Slide 46–50Table 2.4 • p. 104

The professor walks through Table 2.4, an essential diagnostic framework for evaluating collaborative tools across space and time dimensions:

Managers must select collaborative tools that match their team's specific position on the space/time grid.

IT governance & closing synthesis

The lecture closes on IT Governance: the strategy and policies determining decision rights and accountability for an organization's IT investments. Governance dictates who has the authority to approve a multimillion-dollar software purchase, who sets data security rules, and who resolves disputes between departments.
