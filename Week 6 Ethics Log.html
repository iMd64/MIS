Opening and roadmap

Housekeeping, the lecture’s aim and a map of what is coming.

00:00:00to 00:01:32

Getting set up

The professor checks the slides, fills in the attendance documents and confirms the numbering: counting from week zero, this is week six. The topic is ethical and social issues in information systems.

00:01:32to 00:02:53

Information systems in an ordinary day

We use them to communicate, shop, study, manage money and receive services, yet we rarely notice what happens to our information or how a system affects other people.

This week covers the ethical and social side of management information systems. A company could collect a customer’s location all day, but is that always a good idea? The week also covers ownership of digital work, mistakes in computer systems and the effects of technology on daily life.

00:02:53to 00:03:08

The aim: careful decisions

The main goal is to learn how to make careful decisions, not simply how to use new technology.

00:03:08to 00:04:59

Roadmap: three topics, then practice

The lesson runs through three main topics followed by practical work.

  • Problems that appear when information systems become part of society: questions about privacy, power and individual rights.
  • A method for ethical decisions, useful when two important values conflict and there is no perfect answer.
  • Five areas in more detail: personal information, intellectual property, responsibility, system quality and quality of life.
00:04:59to 00:06:28

What you should be able to do

Imagine you are a manager and your company wants to introduce a new application. You should be able to explain what ethical questions it creates, who might benefit and who might be harmed.

Privacy and intellectual property carry weight because digital information is easy to collect and copy. When you describe a system, do not stop at fast, modern or profitable. Ask whether people are treated fairly and whether the system is used responsibly.

Opening case: smart homes for older people

One service that delivers safety and surveillance together.

00:06:28to 00:08:02

Living safely at home

Smart-home technology can help older people stay independent. A sensor detects movement, an emergency button calls for help and a remote door system eases daily tasks.

Now imagine living in a home where your movements are recorded all day. Who receives this information? Do you really agree to the monitoring? Could someone else see your private health details? A useful service can create problems. A responsible design respects the older person’s wishes as well as their safety, and keeps the value of human care.

00:08:02to 00:09:28

Benefits set against concerns

Benefits

  • Help arrives quickly after a fall.
  • A carer can notice a problem earlier.
  • Family members communicate more easily.

Concerns

  • Constant monitoring feels uncomfortable.
  • Some people do not want cameras or sensors in private space.
  • Health data is sensitive and weak security could expose it.
  • Technology should support personal contact, not replace it.
  • Families and cultures disagree about acceptable care.
00:09:28to 00:09:54

Questions to ask before buying for a relative

If you were choosing such a system for a relative, what would you ask before buying? The professor points to consent and to who can access the data, according to the person’s own preferences.

Problems that information systems create

How new systems shift rights and power, the five moral dimensions, and the data practices behind profiling.

00:09:54to 00:11:26

Software changes rights and relationships

The first main topic is understanding the problems information systems create. An organization introduces software to improve a business process, but the change often touches personal rights and relationships. An online platform can give workers flexibility while watching every task they complete, and it can shift the balance of power between managers and employees.

So look past technical performance. What changes for the people who use the system? What new information becomes available? Which rules are needed to prevent unfair treatment?

00:11:26to 00:12:52

Why ethics matters

Technology gives people new abilities, and those abilities can be used in different ways. Customer information can improve a service, or be sold and shared unexpectedly. Managers decide what data to collect, how long to keep it and which employees can see it. These are human decisions, even when software carries them out.

Laws and social expectations take time to catch up, so an action can be technically possible without being clearly accepted. Consider a company that tracks staff locations after working hours. It gains useful information and workers lose privacy. Who gets the benefit and who carries the risk?

00:12:52to 00:14:21

Housekeeping: slides and the reference book

The week has 66 slides, and the professor will explain each. Every topic also appears in the reference book, with its page number at the bottom of each slide. The first slide points to page 154, give or take a page. The slides are short summaries, so go to the book for more.

00:14:21to 00:16:03

Slide 8: information made dishonest

The table on this slide lists cases that are not ordinary computer errors. One concerns a bank manipulating information about interest rates. The others involve unsafe business practices, problems in waste-related reporting and corruption.

The point is shared: information can be hidden, changed or presented dishonestly. A system cannot create ethical behavior by itself, and even an accurate reporting system becomes dangerous when people enter false data or ignore warnings. Managers must build a culture where information is reliable and problems can be reported, because employees pressured to hide bad results will not correct them.

00:16:03to 00:18:08

Three connected levels: person, society, rules

New technology raises questions long before society agrees on answers. A school introduces software that watches students during online exams. First, individual students ask whether that monitoring is fair. As more schools adopt similar tools, parents and teachers start a wider discussion. Eventually lawmakers or education authorities decide formal rules are needed.

That is three levels: ethical questions for individuals, social debate among groups, and political decisions about rules and rights. They affect each other. It also explains why following current law is not always enough: organizations should weigh the effects of their decisions before new regulation forces a change.

00:18:08to 00:20:32

Reading the figure: technology at the center

Information technology sits at the center because it starts many new questions. Moving outward come individuals, society and public policy. A problem can begin with one person’s experience and become important to a whole community.

People lose control over their data, creators have work copied, and organizations disagree about who is responsible for harm. A digital health service that makes a wrong decision touches privacy, safety, responsibility and public trust all at once.

00:20:32to 00:21:59

The five moral dimensions

Five dimensions serve as a checklist for the rest of the chapter. The professor runs one example through all five: a delivery app collects customers’ addresses, uses software owned by developers, makes automatic decisions, depends on reliable data and changes working conditions for drivers.

00:20:41Information rightsWho can collect and use personal data?
00:20:48Property rightsWho owns software, music, designs and other digital creations?
00:20:59AccountabilityWho explains a decision and takes responsibility when something goes wrong?
00:21:09System qualityIs the system accurate, reliable and safe?
00:21:15Quality of lifeWhat are the wider effects on jobs, family, health and society?
00:21:59to 00:23:25

Information rights

Information rights are about a person’s control over information about themselves. A shopping app that asks for your name and delivery address is one thing. Asking for your microphone and contact list is another: would that be necessary?

A company should explain why it collects each item and how it will be used. People need understandable choices, safe storage and a way to correct wrong details. Another key question is whether data collected for one purpose can later be used for something completely different. Good information management means respecting people instead of treating their data as an unlimited resource.

00:23:25to 00:24:58

Property rights

A digital file can be copied in seconds. Taking someone’s printed book without permission is clearly wrong, but copying a digital book, program or photograph can feel less serious because the original stays in place. The creator still spent time and effort making it. Someone who downloads a paid design and uploads it for everyone harms the creator, even though copying is easy.

Organizations need licenses and clear policies so employees know what they may share, change and reuse.

00:24:58to 00:26:32

Accountability

A bank’s system rejects a customer’s payment although the account has enough money. Who should explain? The programmer may have made an error, but other people designed the tests and run the system. A company cannot simply say “the computer decided.”

Accountability means being able to identify decisions and who was responsible for them. That takes records, clear rules and a process for investigating problems. People who are harmed need a way to report the issue and get an answer. Automatic decision-making is useful but must not become an excuse for avoiding responsibility.

00:26:32to 00:27:45

System quality

No large system works perfectly all the time. Developers make mistakes, equipment fails and users enter wrong information. Managers must decide how much testing is needed before people depend on the system, and the answer should be based on risk.

A small mistake in a music app is annoying. A similar mistake in a hospital system could endanger a patient, which makes it an ethical responsibility too. Teams should test important functions, check data quality and prepare for failures. The question is not whether every error can be removed, but whether reasonable steps were taken to protect the people affected.

00:27:45to 00:29:30

Quality of life

Technology makes life more convenient: we attend online classes, talk to relatives abroad and pay without visiting a bank. It can also make people feel they must always be connected and available. Some jobs disappear or change as tasks are automated, and people without good internet access are left behind. The effects are not equal for everyone.

A new platform may save a company money and pressure its workers. Studying quality of life means looking at the full picture: work, health, family, access and fairness. A successful system should improve life for people, not only produce good business numbers.

00:29:30to 00:31:05

Why the stakes keep rising

Several technology changes make ethical decisions more urgent. Computers process more information and storage is cheaper, so organizations can keep customer records for long periods. Better analysis finds patterns in data. Networks move information quickly between companies and countries. Mobile phones collect location and activity during the day.

A hospital can use data to improve care, and the same abilities can support unnecessary surveillance. The main change is the scale of what organizations can know. As their power grows, so does their duty to use information carefully.

00:32:51to 00:34:28

Profiling

Profiling builds a detailed picture of a person from several sources. A supermarket knows what you buy, a website records what you search and a mobile app may hold your location. Each record looks limited, but combined they can reveal habits, interests and sensitive details.

Companies use profiles to recommend products or detect suspicious activity. A profile may also be wrong, and the person may never learn how the conclusion was reached. Imagine getting a different price or service because software placed you in a category: you would want to know why. Profiling raises questions of fairness, data quality, consent and the right to challenge incorrect assumptions.

00:34:28to 00:36:04

NORA: non-obvious relationship awareness

NORA is a method for finding connections that are hard to see when information sits in separate databases. A security team may compare names, phone numbers, transactions and other records to find a possible link between people.

Matching data is not proof of wrongdoing. Two people may share an address or phone number for an innocent reason, and a weak match creates unfair suspicion. Results need human review, good-quality records and clear limits on who can use them. Security benefits do not remove privacy concerns.

00:36:04to 00:37:32

Reading the NORA diagram

Follow the arrows left to right. Separate systems hold watch lists, incident records, customer transactions, telephone information and human resources data. A central process standardizes names and tries to match related records, then raises an alert about a possible connection.

If a person’s name is entered differently in two systems, the process may find the link, or it may confuse two people with similar names. That is why the accuracy of source data matters so much. Understand both the usefulness of the connection and the danger of reaching conclusions too quickly.

A method for ethical decisions

A step-by-step method for decisions where two good values collide, plus six principles to test the answer.

00:37:32to 00:39:02

From problems to decisions

Having seen the kinds of problems, the next question is how to decide well. “I think this is right” is not enough when a decision affects customers, workers and the public. We need clear ideas about responsibility and a step-by-step way to study the situation: separate facts from opinions, identify who is affected, compare actions, think through consequences, and test the choice against ethical principles.

The purpose is not a simple rule for every situation. It is to make decisions we can explain and defend.

00:39:02to 00:40:49

Responsibility, accountability, liability

Two words matter here, and the professor adds a third. An online store shows how they fit: a manager chooses a payment system, the company keeps records of decisions, and the customer may have a legal right if a preventable failure causes a loss. A responsible organization needs all three. “Everyone worked on the project” is not an acceptable explanation of what went wrong.

Responsibility

00:39:16 Accepting that your choices carry duties and possible consequences.

Accountability

00:39:32 A clear way to find out who made the decision.

Liability

00:39:43 Legal responsibility when harm has occurred and someone may seek compensation.

00:40:49to 00:42:16

Due process

Fairness also depends on the process used to decide. People should know the rules that affect them and have a fair way to question a decision. If a university system automatically blocks a student from registering and the student cannot see the reason or reach anyone to fix an error, the process is unfair. The university should explain the rule and offer a clear appeal.

Technology decides quickly, but speed does not guarantee fairness. When a decision has serious effects, people need understandable information and a chance to challenge mistakes.

00:42:16to 00:43:51

The five-step method

A practical method for when ethical problems feel complicated. The running example: a company wants to collect employee location data. First find out why it wants the data and set privacy against safety, then identify workers and managers, consider alternatives and think through what each could cause.

  1. 00:42:26FactsWhat actually happened, and what do we know for sure?
  2. 00:42:38DilemmaName the dilemma and the values in conflict.
  3. 00:42:46StakeholdersList the people or groups the result will affect.
  4. 00:42:57OptionsThink of several realistic options, not the first idea that comes to mind.
  5. 00:43:07ConsequencesCompare what each option leads to, now and later.
00:43:51to 00:45:27

Steps 1 and 2 in practice

Employees say a new app records their location even when they are not working. Before judging the company, check the facts: is the app really active all day, what does the policy say, who can view the information?

Then name the conflict. The company may want to protect equipment or improve delivery planning, while employees want privacy outside working hours. Both concerns can be reasonable, so calling one group good and the other bad solves nothing. Describe the real disagreement in simple terms, and the solutions become easier to compare.

00:45:27to 00:47:01

Steps 3 to 5 in practice

The stakeholders are employees, managers, customers and perhaps the company that supplies the app, each with different needs. The options: turn tracking off completely, collect location only during working hours, or track company vehicles instead of personal phones.

Then weigh consequences. A choice that saves money today might damage trust over several years, while an approach fair to workers may improve cooperation and reduce complaints. Good ethical analysis looks at immediate effects and longer-term relationships.

00:47:01to 00:48:49

Six principles for testing a decision

Ethical principles give different ways to test a choice. The professor lists six questions that can reveal problems. Sometimes two principles point in different directions, so honest judgment is still needed.

00:47:12Golden ruleHow would I feel if I were the person affected?Worked example at 00:48:59. A hidden fee at checkout: would you want to find it only after choosing your products?
00:47:24Kant’s principleWould this still be acceptable if everyone did it?Worked example at 00:49:26. If every shop hid important charges until the last moment, nobody could trust prices.
00:47:35Descartes’ rule of changeWhat happens if this action is repeated again and again?Worked example at 00:50:28. Reading one private message “just this once” becomes a weekly habit, and privacy disappears.

00:48:49to 00:50:22

Golden rule and Kant: the hidden checkout fee

You manage an online store and consider a hidden fee at checkout. The golden rule asks whether you would want to find that fee only after spending time choosing products. Probably not, which shows the customer’s position. Kant’s principle asks what happens if every business hid important charges until the last moment: customers could not trust prices and markets would work less fairly.

The golden rule is about how we treat one person. Kant’s principle is about whether our rule could be followed by everyone. Try applying both to a company that quietly changes its privacy settings.

00:50:22to 00:51:54

Descartes’ rule of change and the utilitarian principle

The rule of change is about repetition. A company reads one employee’s private message without permission because it believes there is an urgent problem. One exception looks harmless, but if the same exception happens every week, employees lose their privacy completely.

The utilitarian principle compares total benefits and costs. An automated service may save many customers time and make access hard for a small group. Consider both results, not just the money saved. Both principles push us past one immediate advantage toward how a decision affects people over time.

00:51:54to 00:53:20

Risk aversion and no free lunch

Risk aversion matters when a decision could cause serious harm. Of two ways to store sensitive medical records, one is cheaper but far more likely to expose information. Even if the problem never happens, the harm would be hard to repair, so the safer option is more responsible.

No free lunch concerns ownership. A useful image, program or document found online may not be copied into a commercial product without permission, because someone created it and may hold rights over its use. Together these two principles encourage caution, protect people from serious risk and respect the value of other people’s work.

00:53:20to 00:55:02

Professional codes of conduct

Doctors, engineers, accountants and computing professionals are trusted because they hold special knowledge, which can help or harm. Codes of conduct set general standards: protect private information, avoid unnecessary damage, respect ownership.

Picture a software engineer who finds a serious security weakness shortly before launch. Pressure to stay silent is real, but professional responsibility means taking the danger seriously. A code cannot answer every hard question and does not replace the law, yet it gives a shared starting point for what a competent, responsible professional should do.

00:55:02to 00:56:52

Critical dilemmas

Real business decisions rarely give a perfect result for everyone. A factory automates and some employees lose jobs. A company monitors its network for security and workers feel watched. Customer data enables useful services and unwanted tracking. Security software spots suspicious behavior and also makes mistakes about innocent people.

These are ethical dilemmas because important values sit on both sides. The job is to recognize the conflict instead of pretending it is absent. A good decision explains which benefits matter, which risks remain and what the organization will do to reduce harm.

00:56:52to 00:58:46

Guided practice: employee internet monitoring

A company wants to reduce security risks and lost working time. Employees say watching their browsing is too personal. Work through the five steps.

  • Write down the facts you would need, such as which websites are recorded and whether workers were told.
  • Identify the values in play: security, productivity, privacy and trust, and the people affected.
  • Suggest at least three options, for example block dangerous sites, monitor only security alerts, or collect wider records under strict limits.
  • Choose one and test it with two ethical principles. Explain who benefits, what risks remain and how the policy should be communicated.
  • Different answers are fine if the reasoning is clear.

Privacy and online tracking

What privacy means, fair information practices, and how cookies, beacons and consent boxes work.

00:58:46to 01:01:01

From the general method to specific areas

The five moral dimensions now become concrete. Next: privacy and how organizations collect personal information online; intellectual property and the ways to protect creative and technical work; responsibility when systems fail and how much system quality is enough; and the wider effects on working life, equal access and health. A manager may not write the code, but still chooses policies, resources and acceptable risks.

01:01:01to 01:02:25

What privacy means

Privacy is more than keeping something secret. People should have a reasonable degree of control over their personal lives and the information about them. A university collects students’ addresses to send documents, then shares them with an advertiser without saying so. Students feel their information was used in a way they never expected.

Organizations see personal data as valuable for decisions and business opportunities. The ethical challenge is deciding where the limits should be. People need to know what is collected, why, and whether they have a meaningful choice.

01:02:25to 01:04:03

Fair information practices

These practices balance the relationship between people and organizations. A person should know when information is collected and what will happen to it, have reasonable choices, and be able to check and correct important records. The organization must protect the data and use it only for the purposes it explains.

A customer who finds a wrong delivery address in an online account should have a simple way to fix it. If private records are exposed because the company ignored security warnings, someone should be responsible. Trust depends on more than a privacy notice. It depends on what the organization actually does.

01:04:03to 01:05:31

Privacy law: United States and Europe

Countries protect privacy in different ways. The textbook compares the United States and Europe, where legal traditions and regulation differ: some approaches target specific industries, others set broad rules for personal information. Legislation changes, so the goal is to understand the management problem, not memorize an old law.

A company offering the same service in several countries may need different procedures for collecting data, explaining concerns and handling complaints. A good manager checks the rules before launching. And the ethical responsibility remains even when an activity is legal.

01:05:31to 01:06:49

What our internet activity reveals

Search terms show our interests, shopping records show habits and repeat visits help a site recognize preferences. Businesses use this for advertising and personalized services: an online store might recommend running shoes after you read several sports pages.

The real issue is whether people understand the collection and have a genuine choice. A long, confusing privacy notice does not create meaningful consent. Imagine seeing the full list of organizations receiving your browsing information. Would you still accept the same tracking?

01:06:49to 01:08:27

Cookies

A cookie is a small piece of information a website sends to a browser. On the next visit the browser can send it back, so the site remembers something about you, such as a shopping basket or a language preference. Cookies can also help companies recognize browsing over time, especially when combined with other data.

The technology is neither kind nor unkind. The purpose matters. Ask whether the cookie is needed for the service or used for advertising and tracking. A responsible site explains important uses and gives people appropriate controls.

01:08:27to 01:10:23

How cookies identify web visitors

The diagram reads as a short conversation between a browser and a web server. This is how a site remembers a user without asking them to re-enter every preference. Recognizing a browser is not always the same as knowing a person’s real identity, but combining the identifier with account or activity records can reveal more.

01:08:51server → browserThe server sends a small identification value. The browser stores it as a cookie.
01:09:08browser → serverOn a later visit, the browser sends that value back.

01:10:23to 01:12:24

Web beacons and less visible tracking

Cookies are not the only method. The chapter also describes web beacons, which can record whether a page or an email was opened. Some tracking is less visible to ordinary users and harder to remove. Opening a promotional email, you may not realize the sender learns about that action.

The problem begins when users do not understand that tracking happens or how the information will be used. The less visible the technology, the greater the need for clear explanations and strong internal rules. Convenience for the company does not make a practice fair.

01:12:24to 01:14:16

Opt-in and opt-out

These are two ways of asking permission. With opt-in, a person actively agrees before information is collected or used. With opt-out, collection may happen unless the person acts to stop it. Signing up for a news site shows the difference, and the way a choice is presented strongly influences what people do.

For consent to be meaningful, the language must be understandable and the alternatives realistic. A confusing button is not proof of informed consent.

Intellectual property

Trade secrets, copyright and patents, and why easy copying does not mean free use.

01:14:16to 01:15:55

From privacy to ownership

Intellectual property means valuable creations that are not physical objects: software, writing, music, design and inventions. They may be easy to copy, yet creating them can take years of work and a great deal of money. Organizations need to protect that investment while letting useful ideas spread.

The chapter introduces three main forms of protection. The central challenge is balancing the interests of creators, businesses and the public.

01:15:55Trade secretBusiness information that gives an advantage because others do not know it.Recipe, production method, customer list. Needs business value and real confidentiality, and is hard to defend once public.
01:17:26CopyrightOriginal expression: writing, music, pictures, computer code.It does not give anyone every use of a general idea. Check the licence, ask permission, give credit.
01:18:44PatentAn invention or process, protected for a limited time.The owner describes the invention and receives exclusive rights. Applications and disputes can be expensive.
01:15:55to 01:17:26

Trade secrets

A trade secret is information that gives a business an advantage because others do not know it: a production method, a customer list, an internal process. The key condition is secrecy. A food company keeps a recipe that only a few authorized employees can access, protected by passwords, confidentiality agreements and limits on who sees the details. If the information becomes public, protection becomes much harder.

It must have business value and be treated as confidential, not just be any private file. Managers decide which information needs protection and how employees handle it.

01:17:26to 01:18:44

Copyright

Copyright protects original expression such as writing, music, pictures and computer code. It does not give one person every possible use of a general idea. Two students may build different apps for booking study rooms with the same basic idea, and each student’s actual code and design remains their own work.

Digital files can be copied perfectly and shared with thousands of people at almost no cost. Check the license or ask permission. Easy access is not the same as free use. Respecting copyright also means crediting creators where required.

01:18:44to 01:20:13

Patents

A patent protects certain inventions or processes for a limited time, under the rules of the relevant patent system. In return for describing the invention, the owner may receive exclusive rights that protect the investment. A company that spent years testing a new technical process benefits, while other companies learn the invention exists.

Patents also bring problems. Applications and disputes can be expensive, and technology often changes faster than legal cases are resolved. Firms must decide when protection is worth the cost. A patent differs from copyright and from a trade secret, so the choice depends on what the company is trying to protect.

01:20:13to 01:22:22

Digital media and the ownership problem

Think of a song, a film or a software program. In digital form one copy can be shared very quickly, often with no loss of quality. People sometimes feel that anything found on the internet belongs to everyone. But access to a file does not give permission to copy, sell or change it, and creators may depend on payment for their work. Businesses use licenses and other controls to say what customers may do.

The professor returns to no free lunch: if somebody creates something valuable, do not assume you can use it without considering their rights.

When systems fail, and what quality means

Who answers for a failure, how much quality is enough, and what systems cost society.

01:22:22to 01:23:37

Who is responsible when a service harms someone

A company’s ordering system sends the wrong medicine to a customer. Perhaps the software had a bug, the database held wrong information or an employee picked the wrong option. More than one organization may have built and run the service. Investigate the actual cause before deciding who carries the cost.

Responsibility concerns duties, accountability helps trace actions and liability concerns legal consequences. Contracts and technical safeguards help, but managers should also give customers a clear way to report serious problems.

01:23:37to 01:25:42

How good does a system need to be?

Complex programs cannot be tested in every situation, and every extra test costs time and money. Releasing too early can cost far more than it saves. A minor display error in a video game can be fixed in a later update. A similar error in a system that calculates medical doses has far more serious consequences.

Managers should weigh the risk, the number of people affected and whether the harm can be corrected. Quality decisions should follow the importance of the system, not only a planned release date.

01:25:42to 01:27:03

Three reasons systems perform badly

A software problem such as a programming mistake. A hardware or facility failure, such as a server stopping or a power problem interrupting service. And poor input data: a university database holds the wrong exam mark, and even flawless software calculates wrong final results.

This last case is often called garbage in, garbage out. A useful system needs more than correct code. It needs accurate records, sensible checks, reliable equipment and procedures for correcting mistakes once someone finds them.

01:27:03to 01:28:25

Wider social costs

Information systems save time, create jobs and connect people. They can also increase pressure at work, change who holds power and make life harder for people without digital skills. A service may get cheaper after moving online while older customers struggle if there is no other way to get help.

When managers measure success they should look beyond efficiency and profit. What does the change mean for families, workers, communities and people who find it hard to access the service?

01:28:25to 01:29:52

Power and speed

Systems can change who holds power in an organization. Sharing information across departments lets more employees make decisions, while a large platform that controls important customer data may gain strong influence over smaller businesses. Technology can spread power or concentrate it, depending on how it is managed.

Speed is a second challenge. Businesses launch new processes quickly and competitors respond almost immediately, leaving workers little time to learn. Replacing a familiar process with a new platform in one week may create stress and mistakes even if the platform is better. Good change management gives people time, training and support.
