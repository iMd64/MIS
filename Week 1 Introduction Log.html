The MIS perspective: people, process, data & technology

Why MIS is not about memorizing software names, the socio-technical lens, and business-technology alignment.

00:04:30to 00:07:30Slide 01–03Guide § 1 • p. 1

What MIS is—and what it is not

Dr. Kenan delivers the foundational definition of the course: MIS is not a course about memorizing software brand names or coding syntax. Instead, it investigates how organizations design, use, govern, and improve digital systems so that operational work, managerial decisions, and corporate strategy become more effective.

Many organizations waste millions of dollars purchasing fashionable enterprise tools that fail to create value because management treats technology as an isolated fix. An information system only creates value when it integrates four interconnected dimensions:

00:05:40PeopleRoles, skills, user incentives, judgment, accountability, and organizational culture.
00:06:10ProcessRepeatable workflows, handoffs between departments, business rules, and internal controls.
00:06:40DataDefinitions, accuracy, real-time availability, security, and governance lifecycles.
00:07:05TechnologyHardware, software, telematics, cloud infrastructure, enterprise APIs, and security tools.
00:07:30to 00:10:45Slide 03Guide § 1 • p. 2

The core analytical habit: problem first, technology second

The professor highlights the primary bad habit of junior engineers and managers: jumping straight to software selection before understanding the business reality. The standard trap is asking, “Which AI tool or cloud service should we buy?”

An elite MIS analyst reverses the inquiry: What specific organizational problem are we solving? Who are the affected stakeholders? What business processes are broken? What data is required? What risks exist? And how will we measure success? Every organization, from a neighborhood café managing milk inventory to a major university processing exam grades, depends on this fundamental system logic.

Starting point & student experience poll

Student background check, bridging software engineering and business logic, and mutual conversation expectations.

00:10:45to 00:14:15Slide 02Poll • p. 2

The student experience poll: bridging technical and managerial minds

Dr. Kenan polls the virtual room regarding prior background in databases, programming, accounting, and business process modeling. He explains that students with strong software development backgrounds often over-focus on code efficiency, while students from management backgrounds often underestimate technical integration friction.

Engineering / Technical Perspective

  • Focuses on server uptime, algorithmic latency, and architecture.
  • Assumes users will follow system logic if software works correctly.
  • Treats change as a technical deployment problem.

MIS / Socio-Technical Perspective

  • Focuses on workflow bottlenecks, user incentives, and decision quality.
  • Recognizes that politics and routines cause workers to resist new software.
  • Treats change as organizational redesign supported by digital tools.

The goal of the course is to turn technical students into strategic architects who can communicate fluently with CFOs and business executives.

The semester team project: problem, evidence & feasibility

Guidelines for the capstone team project: selecting a narrow problem, gathering real evidence, and avoiding superficial solutions.

00:25:35to 00:29:40Slide 08Guide § 3 • p. 3

The semester project mandate: real problems over artificial tools

Dr. Kenan outlines the expectations for the semester team project. Rather than inventing an imaginary high-tech startup with unrealistic claims, student teams must identify a specific, narrow organizational bottleneck in an existing business or public service.

Examples of credible targets include: pharmacy prescription verification delays, retail warehouse inventory mismatch, clinic appointment scheduling friction, or university graduation clearance handoffs. The project requires following a strict analytical lifecycle:

Phase 1Problem Definition
Document current workflow pain point.
Phase 2Empirical Evidence
Collect process logs, timing, and error rates.
Phase 3System Requirements
Map functional needs across People, Process, Data.
Phase 4Solution Architecture
Design feasible digital tool with integration specs.
Phase 5Governance & Roadmap
Define security, change plan, and milestone schedule.
00:29:40to 00:33:30Slide 08Deliverables

Deliverables & what will NOT earn marks

The professor issues an explicit warning against superficial submissions: submitting generic slide decks containing broad slogans (“we will use AI and blockchain to make the company better”) will result in failure.

High-scoring project reports must include concrete, evidence-based artifacts: an as-is versus to-be process flow diagram (BPMN), a relational data entity dictionary, a screen prototype or dashboard mock-up, an explicit risk assessment matrix, and a phased rollout budget. Every proposal must justify its business return on investment.

First applied activity, data ethics & Week 1 preview

Everyday digital touchpoints, synthetic data rules, academic integrity with AI, and preview of Week 1.

00:41:20to 00:45:50Slide 11–13Activity • p. 5

First practical exercise: dissecting an everyday digital action

Dr. Kenan assigns the first thinking activity: choose an ordinary digital interaction you performed today—ordering food on a mobile app, scanning a transit card, or reserving library study space—and deconstruct it across all four MIS dimensions.

  • People: Who initiated the action, who fulfills it, and who is accountable if it fails?
  • Process: What sequence of validations, checks, and approvals occurred in the background?
  • Data: What records were generated, modified, stored, and sent to downstream dashboards?
  • Technology: What client devices, networks, payment gateways, and cloud servers communicated?

This exercise transitions students from passive consumers of software into active system analysts who perceive the organizational machinery underneath digital interfaces.

00:45:50to 00:49:10PolicyEthics & AI

Data ethics, synthetic records & artificial intelligence rules

The professor issues strict governance rules regarding student research and course project data: under no circumstances should student teams extract, store, or share private personal data, customer banking information, or confidential company secrets.

Teams must use publicly released datasets, synthetic dummy data, anonymized records, or officially sanctioned academic case files. Furthermore, while AI tools may assist in drafting ideas, copying unverified AI text directly into exam papers or project deliverables without attribution is strictly prohibited and constitutes academic dishonesty.

Process Dimension00:06:10The coordinated set of logical tasks, standard operating procedures, handoffs, and controls that transform business inputs into value-added outputs.
Data Dimension00:06:40The raw facts, observations, and structured records managed throughout their lifecycle to guarantee accuracy, timeliness, and security for decision-makers.
Technology Dimension00:07:05The physical hardware, system software, relational databases, telecommunications networks, and cloud services supporting organizational workflows.

Systems Thinking00:13:10An analytical method that evaluates interdependencies, inputs, outputs, boundaries, and feedback loops across the entire firm rather than optimizing isolated departments.
